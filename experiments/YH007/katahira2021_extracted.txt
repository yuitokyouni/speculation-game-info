Abstract

We sophisticate the Speculation Game to relax the need of rigorous tuning of a
part of parameters for the emergence of characteristic statistics of price returns.
Named as Self-organized Speculation Game (SOSG), thanks to the concept of
the Bak-Tang-Wiesenfeld (BTW) sandpile model, the model can spontaneously
adjust the number of market participants during the process of reaching the
quasi-critical state. While the original Speculation Game's high reproducibility
of financial stylized facts is maintained, the behavioral characteristics of this
modified model, such as the dynamics of system size and the observations of
power-law phenomena, strongly resemble those of BTW model. The market
size is evolutionally settled with fluctuations within a certain range, similar to
the total sand grains behave in the sandpile model. SOSG infers the possibility
that, in the real financial markets, it could be the self-organized quasi-criticality
which works behind the spontaneous emergence of universal financial stylized
facts.
Keywords: Financial stylized facts, Self-organized criticality, Speculation
Game, BTW sandpile model, Quasi-critical state

    1. Introduction

    1.1. Background

         Time series of price returns in financial markets are known to have sev-
    eral quantitative and qualitative statistical properties collectively called styl-
5 ized facts. Cont reported 11 representative statistical properties [1] including
    the well-known volatility clustering, which is characterized by the tendency for
    the large scale price returns to cluster together [2, 3, 4]. Those features are
    quite nontrivial because they can be observed universally in different markets
    and instruments.
10 In the studies of econophysics, several emerging mechanisms for volatility
    clustering have been proposed based on bottom-up simulations employing sim-
    ple agent-based models. The most popular, and maybe convincing one as well,
    is the effect of herding behavior [5, 6], which refers to the intermittent synchro-
    nization of actions among traders. For example, based on studies with the family
15 of Minority Game [7] typed models, one may infer that either a combination
    of inductive learning with dynamic capitals [8, 9] or a performance-dependent
    switch between active and inactive strategies [3, 10], could trigger the herd-
    ing behaviors among the players and result in large-scaled intermittent price
    fluctuations. Another plausible mechanism is the order-book price formation.
20 Typically, the Maslov model [11] suggests that the continuous double auction
    system alone can cause the clustering of volatile price fluctuations. As the latest
    study, we recently proposed alternative market scenarios with some empirical
    evidence in the study of the Speculation Game [12]. Specifically, it is found that
    heterogeneous holding periods in round-trip trades may agitate volatilities by
25 broadly differentiated trading volumes caused by the diversification of capital
    among traders [13].

         Despite these progresses in revealing the potential mechanism for volatility
    clustering, little is known about the spontaneous emergence of stylized facts in
    the financial markets. Stylized facts can be commonly observed irrespective
30 of the types of markets, the lengths of histories, the differences in regulations,

                                                        2
    and so on. For instance, similar statistics of price returns can be observed
    either in the mature markets with histories of more than 100 years (such like
    US markets) or in the emerging markets with at most 30 years of history (such
    like Chinese or Indian markets). The inference of such a universal spontaneity
35 is that the number of parameters of market models that need to be artificially
    tuned should be very small. In other words, a certain kind of spontaneous
    adjustment mechanism should work in the background. However, even the most
    recent market models, including the Speculation Game, essentially require the
    external fine-tuning of all control parameters for the reproduction of stylized
40 facts.

         In econophysics, one possible approach to addressing this problem is to con-
    ceptually make the assumption that the market system must stay in a nearly
    critical state [6, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18], which might be supported by two primary
    aspects of stylized facts. First, the empirical (complementary) cumulative dis-
45 tributions of positive and negative price returns display power-laws in the tail
    part, with a finite exponents  (2 <  < 5) [2, 19, 20]. Second, the auto-
    correlation of volatilities decays slowly with the time interval, which may be
    fitted either by a power-law [1, 19] or by a logarithmic function [21]. In physics,
    criticality is defined without ambiguity as the state of a system in which the
50 frequency distribution of some event demonstrates a power-law tail. Simulta-
    neously, spatial and/or temporal correlation functions should also decay with a
    power law, indicating the existence of infinite correlation lengths [22]. On the
    other hand, the decay of autocorrelation of volatilities in some markets or in-
    struments follow the logarithmic function rather than the power law [23], hence
55 it should be more appropriate to categorize the actual state of financial markets
    as the quasi-critical state instead of a pure critical one.

         Regarding the process approaching the critical or quasi-critical states, there
    is a unique physical principle called self-organized criticality (SOC) that allows
    self-adjustment of a parameter corresponding to the system condition1 [24, 25].

         1A mechanism called sweeping of instability has also been proposed by Sornette et al.

60 In 1987, Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld (BTW) proposed the SOC concept elab-
    orated with the famous sandpile model as a non-equilibrium system that can
    reach the critical state under quite general conditions [26]. In a two-dimensional
    BTW sandpile model, a sand grain falls on a randomly chosen site in an L × L
    lattice which causes a unit increase of the slope of that site. If the local value

65 of slope got larger than a threshold, a landslide would occur causing the in-
    crease of slope in the neighbor sites. A chain of those landslides brings about
    an avalanche, through which sand grains roll down to the edge and fall off the
    lattice. Even such a set of simple rules can render the system critical, that
    is, the emergence of a power-law relationship between the frequency and size

70 of avalanches. Note that there are SOC systems which can only reach quasi-
    critical states, such as the Olami-Feder-Christensen (OFC) earthquake model
    [27]. Consequently, the behavior of such a system is categorized as the self-
    organized quasi-criticality (SOqC) [28].
         To the best of authors' knowledge, there are few agent-based financial mar-

75 ket models equipped with the SOC setup. The Alfi-Pietronero-Zaccaria (APZ)
    market model [29, 30, 31], in which financial stylized facts are explained as the
    finite size effects, enables the autoregulation of the number of active agents with
    thresholds of realized volatility for entering and exiting the markets. On the
    other hand, the contagion financial pricing (CFP) model [32], in which the effect

80 of an information cascade is involved, is indeed a sophisticated version of the
    financial quakes model [17, 33], where traders' imitating behavior of pricing is
    introduced by using the structure of the OFC earthquake model. Whereas basic
    stylized facts can be recovered with these two models, a large number of pa-
    rameter values need to be specified in both models, which prevents a persuasive

85 explanation for the universal power law behaviors in financial markets.

    1.2. Purpose

         In this study, a simple agent-based model equipped with the SOC principle
    is developed for a straight exploration of the spontaneous emerging of financial
    stylized facts. In particular, the "Self-organized Speculation Game" (SOSG) is
90 constructed with a direct use of the structure of BTW sandpile model. The
    model enables the spontaneous adjustment of market size without losing the
    baseline Speculation Game model's high reproducibility of stylized facts. Fur-
    thermore, the behavioral commonalities between the BTW sandpile model and
    the SOSG shows the possibility that SOqC may work behind the spontaneity
95 of emergence for financial stylized facts.

         The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the
    model, and Section 3 presents the results and discussion. Finally, Section 4
    concludes.

     2. Self-organized Speculation Game

     different from the gain or loss calculated directly based on the market price,
135 though the difference is assumed to be adjusted externally; in other words, self-
     finance is not postulated in the SOSG. Lastly, if the wealth of a player decreases
     to the level wi(t) < B, making her impossible to order even one unit of quantity,
     the player will be forced to leave the market. Hence, B also works as a significant
     threshold value to induce irregular withdrawals of players, which is analogous
140 with the fact that the threshold dynamics play an important role in the BTW
     sandpile model.

         To emphasize, the major difference in the structure of SOSG from that of
     the standard Speculation Game is the reduction of two main control parameters:
     the number of players N and the number of players' strategies S. Instead of
145 the fixed population size with substitutions for bankruptcies in the Speculation
     Game, N in the SOSG can change and eventually be settled through a constant
     inflow of a player to a gamified market and irregular outflows of bankrupted
     players from it. Additionally, the value of S is set as one, considering the
     finding in our previous study [13] that inductive learning is not indispensable to
150 the reproduction of stylized facts with strategy tables in the Speculation Game.

     3. Results and discussions

     3.1. Market size self-adjustment
         First, we summarize that the market size can be spontaneously adjusted in

     SOSG along with the reproduction of volatility clustering in the price returns.
155 Figs.1 and 2 display the time series of the number of players participating in

     the gamified market and the corresponding price returns r(t), respectively. The
     three main parameters of the SOSG are set as M = 5, B = 9, and C =
     3 by following the baseline setting of the Speculation Game in [12] and [13].
     Henceforth, the memory size and the cognitive threshold are fixed as M = 5
160 and C = 3. In Fig.1, the number of players surges in the beginning of the
     simulation and subsequently fluctuates around approximately 700 players. In
     the meantime, Fig.2 shows that the magnitudes of r(t) fluctuate intermittently,
     indicating the stable emergence of volatility clustering.

         Note that the SOSG is equivalent to the original Speculation Game [12] re-
165 garding the reproducibility of stylized facts (10 out of 11 stylized facts in Cont's

     report [1] are confirmed except for the gain/loss asymmetry [35]). Additionally,
     the emerging mechanism of volatility clustering and the effects of the parameters
     in SOSG are identical to those elucidated in [13] as well. Furthermore, similar to
     the Perturbated Speculation Game [36], the introduction of perturbation to the
170 SOSG price change, which represents the effects of non-pure speculators, can
     generate price trends so that the correct Hurst exponent can be obtained and
     thus the long term scaling of fluctuation is ensured. Therefore, by maintaining
     the basic features of the Speculation Game, the relaxation in the requirement of
     a predetermined market size suggests that SOSG can be considered a smarter
175 model that abstracts more essential elements of the financial market.

     3.2. Commonalities with the BTW sandpile model
     3.2.1. Spontaneous settlement of system size

         In the following sections, we show that the behavior of the BTW sandpile
     model and SOSG are quite similar. First, both models have the characteristic
180 that the final system size, to which the required time length for adjustment is
     proportional, is highly determined by a single control parameter. In the BTW
     sandpile model, the number of sand grains that remain on the lattice depends
     on the length of its side L as indicated in Fig.3. Lager L does not only increase
     the number of retained sand grains but also lengthens necessary time steps to
185 reach stable states. Similarly in the SOSG, the number of players staying in
     the gamified market strongly hinges on the value of the board lot amount B.
     A larger B, implying more difficult market conditions for players to place large
     orders, results in a larger market size and a longer time for the convergence, as
     indicated in Fig.4. However, the fluctuations in leaving players in SOSG during
190 the stable state are a bit larger than those in the outflowing sand grains in a
     BTW sandpile model.

     3.3. Reasons for spontaneous market-size settlement
         The spontaneous settlement of system size under SOC in the BTW sand-

     pile model has been explained with a general concept called the absorbing
245 phase transition, which is presented in the theory of phase transition for non-

     equilibrium systems [41]. The absorbing phase transition is defined as a system-
     atic changeover from the active phase, during which the reaction never stops
     and continuously changes the structure, to the absorbing state, namely, a state
     in which the system is trapped forever with a monotonous structure once it is
250 reached. Because the separation of timescales in the BTW sandpile model al-
     lows the unlimited dissipation of sand grains, the particle density on the lattice
     is ensured to recover its critical value for which the absorbing phase transition
     occurs even though the slow addition of a grain will drive the system back to the
     active phase. With such a mechanism leading the system to the critical point
255 acts as an attractor for the system dynamics, which can stabilize the number of
     sand grains remaining on the lattice.

         Intuitively, the size settlement in SOSG should also have some relationship
     with the absorbing phase transition. However, the direct explanation shown
     above is not applicable to SOSG, because the "lattice" size of the gamified

                                                        15
260 market that serves as a physical space for the residence of SOSG players is

not fixed. In contrast, the lattice that similarly functions as a receiver of sand

grains in the BTW sandpile model is invariant. Furthermore, if the exchange

or redistribution of wealth among the SOSG player could be loosely defined as

a kind of local "dissipation", surely the rate of such a dissipation would not

265 be unlimited due to the lack of catenated responses. Hence, we can observe a

larger fluctuations in the time evolution of that number of players than that of

sand grains.

in the stable state. In the case of the BTW sandpile model, the difference

to the total deposit of sand grain, which is the accumulation of the falling sand

     market. Consequently, an equation similar to Eq.7 can also be established,
     showing the balance in the averaged number of entering and exiting players.

310 4. Conclusion

         In this paper, the SOC mechanism is incorporated into the Speculation Game
     for the realization of spontaneous adjustments of the market size in the process
     of generating stylized financial properties. Compared with the original model,
     the established SOSG succeeds in reducing the number of main parameters from
315 five (N , S, M , B, C) to three (M , B, C) and maintains the high reproducibil-
     ity of 10 stylized facts. In this sense, the SOSG model can be regarded as an
     essential type of Speculation Game. Moreover, our study reveals that SOSG
     shares several common phenomenological characteristics with the BTW sand-
     pile model: the spontaneous settlement of system size, distributions following
320 the power laws, and the balance (in the time-averaging sense) between system
     inflows and outflows. On the other hand, the SOSG system can reach merely a
     subcritical state in reference with the non power-law decay of the autocorrela-
     tion in volatilities. Additionally, the stabilizing mechanism of system size in the

                                                        19
     SOSG is slightly more complex because the averaged number of inflowing and
325 outflowing players in the gamified market is essentially related to the averaged

     amount of inflowing and outflowing market wealth behind.
         Results of our study suggest the potential existence of SOqC in the actual

     financial markets as well as its possible contribution to the spontaneous emer-
     gence of stylized facts. The two essential premises here are that the financial
330 markets are open systems and that capital flows through the entrance and the
     exit of market participants. It is also notable that SOSG does not require the
     spacial structure, or local interaction, for the emergence of SOqC, which is sig-
     nificantly different from the settings of the BTW sandpile model.
